Pythagoras' Theorem

a²+b²=c²

In a right triangle, the square on the longest side equals the sum of the squares on the other two.

The idea

Three sides, one rule.

Take any triangle with one square corner. Measure its two shorter sides, square each number, and add them. The total is exactly the square of the longest side. This works for every right triangle, large or small.

Meet the sides

a and b are the legs. They meet at the 90° corner.

c is the hypotenuse. It sits opposite the right angle and is always the longest side.

"Squared" means a number times itself. A side of 3 gives a square whose area is 3 × 3 = 9. The theorem is really a statement about area: the two small squares, put together, cover exactly the same area as the big one.

Try it on the right. Slide the legs and watch the areas. Blue plus red always equals the grey square, to the last digit.

How it's built

A proof you can see.

There are hundreds of proofs. This one uses nothing but four identical triangles and two big squares, and it is easy to follow with your eyes alone.

3
4

Step 1: two equal squares

Both big squares have side a + b, so both have the same area, (a + b)².

Step 2: same four triangles

Each big square holds four copies of the triangle. Left, they leave a tilted square of side c. Right, they leave two squares, a² and b².

Step 3: take them away

Remove the same four triangles from both. What remains must have equal area, so c² = a² + b².

History

Older than Pythagoras.

The rule carries his name, but people in several places knew it long before he lived. Open each stop to read more.

c. 1800 BCE

Babylon

Clay tablets with right-triangle numbers.

The tablet Plimpton 322 lists number sets that behave like Pythagorean triples. Another tablet, YBC 7289, gives the diagonal of a square with side 1 as 1;24,51,10 in base 60. That is about 1.41421296, very close to √2.

1;24,51,10 (base 60) = 1 + 24/60 + 51/3600 + 10/216000 ≈ 1.41421
Mesopotamia, today's Iraq
c. 800 BCE

India: the Sulba Sutras

Altars built with the rule.

Baudhayana's Sulba Sutra states that the rope along the diagonal of a rectangle makes an area equal to the two areas made by the rope along its length and its breadth. Priests used this to lay out the precise shapes of Vedic fire altars, and listed triples such as 3-4-5 and 5-12-13.

Indian subcontinent
c. 570 – 495 BCE

Pythagoras of Samos

The name that stuck.

Pythagoras founded a school of mathematics, music and philosophy at Croton in southern Italy. Later Greek writers credit him with the first proof. None of his own writing survives, so what he personally worked out is uncertain. His followers gave the theorem his name.

Greece and southern Italy
c. 300 BCE

Euclid's Elements

The classic written proof.

In Book I, Proposition 47, Euclid proves the theorem using areas of squares and triangles. Proposition 48 proves the reverse: if c² = a² + b², the triangle must have a right angle.

Alexandria, Egypt
c. 200 BCE – 3rd century CE

China: the gougu rule

A diagram with a proof by rearranging.

The text Zhoubi Suanjing describes the "gou-gu" rule using a 3-4-5 triangle. In the 3rd century, Liu Hui wrote a commentary that proves it by cutting and rearranging squares, much like the proof in this page.

China
c. 1150 CE

Bhaskara II

A proof with one word: "Behold!"

The Indian mathematician Bhaskara II gave a rearrangement proof of the theorem. A famous account says his diagram was captioned simply "Behold!", trusting the picture to do the explaining.

India
1876

James Garfield's proof

A future US president adds one.

James A. Garfield, then a member of the US Congress and later president, published a proof based on the area of a trapezoid. Elisha Loomis's 1927 book The Pythagorean Proposition collects 370 different proofs.

Real life

Six places it quietly works.

Any time two straight lines meet at a right angle and you need the length of the slanted line between their ends, this theorem does the work. Move the controls in each picture.

1 · Home and work

Placing a ladder

A 5 m ladder leans on a wall. The ground distance d and the wall height h form the legs, and the ladder is the hypotenuse, so h = √(5² − d²). Move the foot of the ladder to see how high it reaches.

2 · Screens

TV and phone sizes

A screen is sold by its diagonal. The real width and height are the legs. For a ratio of w : h, the width is diagonal × w ÷ √(w² + h²). Pick a size and shape.

3 · Getting around

The shortcut across a field

Walking along two sides of a rectangular ground covers a + b. Cutting across the diagonal covers √(a² + b²), which is always shorter. Change the ground and see how much you save.

4 · Building

Squaring a corner with 3-4-5

Builders mark 3 units along one wall and 4 along the other. If the distance between the marks is exactly 5, the corner is a perfect 90°. Drag the measured diagonal and watch the angle.

5 · Access

Designing a ramp

A ramp must climb a set height with a gentle slope. A slope of 1 : 12 means 12 cm of run for every 1 cm of rise. The ramp surface itself is the hypotenuse, which tells you how much material to buy.

6 · Maps and games

Distance between two points

Maps, GPS and video games find the straight-line distance between two points the same way. Subtract the x values and the y values to get the legs, then use √(dx² + dy²). Drag either point.

A note

From the maker.

I made this page to understand a² + b² = c² by seeing it, not by memorising it. The proof clicked for me when I moved the same four triangles around and the leftover areas matched.

Now I notice right angles everywhere: a ladder, a screen, a ramp, a path across a ground. One small rule, and it measures all of them.
